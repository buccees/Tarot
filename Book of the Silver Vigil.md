# The Silver Vigil (Chapter One)

## The first thing he remembered was the screaming.

It wasn't his own. He had learned long ago to ignore his own pain. It was the screaming of the people in the street below the temple that had torn him from his sleep, followed by the sound of bells hammering through the darkness. He had risen from his bed and gone to the window, expecting fire in one of the poorer districts—perhaps a warehouse along the docks or another tavern gone up in flames.

Instead, he had seen the moon.

It hung above Baldur's Gate, enormous and pale behind the smoke, and beneath it, the eastern quarter of the city was coming apart. People ran through the streets with no direction, some carrying children, others dragging the wounded behind them. Horses screamed in their harnesses. Somewhere below, a building collapsed with a thunderous roar, sending sparks high into the night. The temple bells had not stopped ringing, and from beyond the city came a sound he knew too well to mistake for anything human: a wolf's howl.

He had stood at the window for only a moment before reaching for his sword.

Raised in the Temple of the Silver Vigil, Bastian knew the Moonwardens did not waste time wondering whether trouble might come to them. They went looking for it. His family had served Selûne for generations, though "service" was too gentle a word for what had been expected of them. The Moonwardens were hunters, and their quarry was the cursed, the transformed, and those creatures that wore the shape of men until the moon called something darker from within them.

He had been taught their trade almost as soon as he could walk. His childhood was measured in lessons of tracking, restraint, and endurance. By the time other boys were learning how to read, he was learning how to tell whether the blood upon a doorstep belonged to a man or a beast. It had never seemed particularly strange to him; it was simply what his family did, and he had never hated it. The work was dangerous, but it paid well enough to make the danger tolerable. He had an appreciation for good craftsmanship, and the work of a Moonwarden provided ample opportunity to acquire it. A fine sword was worth its price. Good armor meant the difference between a scar and a funeral. A comfortable inn was preferable to a ditch, and a well-prepared meal was worth more than most sermons.

He had no shame about wanting such things. He simply preferred a life in which he could afford them.

That life depended upon another lesson his family had taught him just as carefully: no one could know what he truly did.

The Moonwardens worked best when nobody knew they were present. A creature warned that a hunter was looking for it became difficult to find, and a witch who knew her coven had been discovered would simply vanish. The work required patience, deception, and the ability to walk into a town as an ordinary man and leave it before anyone realized why he had come.

So he had become an adventurer. That was the name the world gave him, and he had never found reason to correct it. He traveled, took contracts, explored old ruins, and spent his earnings rather more freely than most adventurers dared. He was a cleric of Selûne—or at least that much was true. What no one knew was that beneath the wandering life and the expensive equipment was a Moonwarden following trails other men could not see.

It had been a comfortable arrangement. Until the witches.

He remembered the battle now in pieces: the smell of rain upon the earth, the strange blue light burning between the trees, and one of the witches laughing as though the fight were some private amusement. He remembered the sudden pain in his shoulder when her magic struck him, and the fleeting thought that she had missed—until he saw the mark the spell had left behind.

He had not understood what she had done. Not then. By the time he did, it was too late. The curse had taken hold before dawn.

What followed became the reason he would never again be welcome beneath the roof of the Silver Vigil. He had returned to Baldur's Gate believing the temple could cleanse him. He knew the rites and the prayers, having watched them performed upon others and having assisted in more than one cleansing himself. Ordinary lycanthropy was not beyond the reach of the Moonwardens, and he had no reason to believe his case would be different.

He had been wrong.

The priests had gathered around him beneath the silvered dome of the temple, calling upon Selûne to drive the curse from his flesh. The first rite failed. The second did nothing. The third brought such agony that he nearly tore himself free of the restraints.

Then the beast came.

The faces of the priests were what remained with him most clearly. They had seen the wolf before; they knew fear, but they knew how to master it. This time, however, they were looking at something they did not understand. The witch's curse had twisted the lycanthropy into something beyond their knowledge. Their magic could not control it, and neither could he. When the transformation came, there was no prayer strong enough to stop it and no chain heavy enough to hold him.

The eastern quarter of Baldur's Gate had paid the price.

He had awakened afterward with blood upon his clothes and smoke filling the sky, not knowing how many people had died. He remembered running through streets he had known since childhood, remembered men shouting his name, and remembered Moonwardens closing in around him—the very people who had taught him how to hunt, now coming to hunt him. He had escaped only because he knew precisely how they would pursue him. Every trap, every formation, every expected route was one he had practiced himself.

When it was over, the elders gave him his sentence: exile.

They did not call him evil, nor did they accuse him of choosing the beast. Somehow, that made it worse. They simply told him he could no longer remain. He had understood. If another Moonwarden had returned carrying such a curse, he would have argued for the same decision.

Now, several days later, he rode hard along a forest road, the smoke of Baldur's Gate still visible behind him. His horse was exhausted, and so was he, but neither could afford to stop. The Moonwardens would eventually come for him. Perhaps they already had.

Yet he was not running merely to escape. He had a destination.

Before leaving the city, he had heard a rumor from a frightened merchant whose daughter had once disappeared into the western forests: beyond the old ruins lived a coven of witches who practiced magic older than the temples and dealt in curses ordinary priests could not undo. Under other circumstances, Bastian might have been sent to hunt them. Now, he intended to ask them for help.

The thought struck him as absurd, but he refused to surrender the life he had built. He wanted his life back. Baldur's Gate had been his home, but the Temple of the Silver Vigil was where he belonged. Exile only meant he had something to correct before he could go home.

The Search Begins
The first part of the journey was simple enough. Bastian kept to merchant roads, avoiding rough lodgings in favor of comfortable rooms where his coin still held weight. But the greater problem was information: he knew what he was looking for, but not where to begin. The temple's records offered no insight into his specific affliction, leaving him no choice but to track down the witches themselves.

For several days, he listened more than he spoke in roadside inns and crowded taprooms. Most local tales were worthless—ordinary fears turning foxes into demons and sick neighbors into witches. But eventually, a conversation caught his attention. Two men were arguing over a northern road where a merchant caravan had been found weeks earlier. The horses were frightened, the wagons untouched, and the guards dead.

"Bandits?" someone had asked.
"Bandits leave tracks," another replied. There weren't any.

More disturbingly, one of the caravan guards had eventually gone home. His wife swore it was him—same face, same voice, same clothes—but she wouldn't let him inside the house.

The story reminded Bastian too sharply of the aftermath in Baldur's Gate: a curse that changed things, leaving people unable to recognize what had once been familiar. Changing his route the next morning, he rode north into increasingly wild country until he reached the site of the caravan. The wagons were gone, but near an old, rain-washed fire pit at the edge of the trees, he found a small piece of finely worked metal half-buried in the damp earth. He slipped it into his pouch.

By late afternoon, he reached a small settlement and found shelter at a local inn. Asking around with practiced casualness, he learned that the missing guard wasn't the only disappearance along that stretch of road—a farmer had vanished the previous spring, a peddler the year before, and a traveler had recently been found wandering near the marsh with no memory of where he had been.

When Bastian asked the innkeeper about the marsh, she gave him a long, measuring look.
"Why?" she asked.
"I am traveling north," Bastian replied.
"That isn't what I asked."
"No," he said, "but it is the answer I have."

The woman almost smiled, sliding a small clay cup of wine across the counter. "You'll want to speak with Harven before you go farther north. He trades herbs along the old road. Knows the marsh better than anyone."

The next morning, Bastian found Harven in a weathered cottage beside a narrow stream. The old herb trader was reluctant to speak, but Bastian's directness eventually broke through.

"You've been cursed," Harven said, studying the cleric's guarded posture.
"Why would you say that?" Bastian asked quietly.
"Because you have the look of a man searching for an answer rather than a criminal."

There was no point in lying. "Do you know anything about witches?"
"I know enough to stay away from them," Harven replied, looking toward the window.
"That isn't what I asked."
"No," the old man sighed, "but it is the advice I would give you. There are people in the marsh who know things... Years ago, a woman came through asking questions about curses. She wasn't from here, and she didn't stay long."
"Where did she go?"
"I don't know. But you're not the first person to ask. A traveler came through not long ago, asking many of the same questions. He was looking for someone in the marsh, too."
"Did you tell him anything?"
"I told him what I knew: that if he wanted answers, he should stop looking for them on the road." Harven's gaze shifted toward the door. "He left heading north."

Bastian thanked him, mounted his horse, and looked out toward the road beyond the cottage. Somewhere ahead lay the marsh, and somewhere beyond that were people who might finally hold the key to breaking his curse.

The trail was faint, but it was a trail. Bastian turned his horse north and followed it.
