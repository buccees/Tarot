# The Silver Vigil

## Chapter One

The first thing he remembered was the screaming.

Not his own. He had learned long ago to ignore his own pain. It was the screaming of the people in the street below the temple that had torn him from his sleep, followed by the sound of bells hammering through the darkness. He had risen from his bed and gone to the window expecting to see fire in one of the poorer districts, perhaps a warehouse along the docks or another tavern gone up in flames. Instead, he had seen the moon.

It hung above Baldur's Gate, enormous and pale behind the smoke, and beneath it the eastern quarter of the city was coming apart.

People ran through the streets with no direction, some carrying children, others dragging the wounded behind them. Horses screamed in their harnesses. Somewhere below, a building collapsed with a thunderous roar, sending sparks high into the night. The temple bells had not stopped ringing, and from beyond the city came a sound he knew too well to mistake for anything human.

A wolf's howl.

He stood at the window for only a moment before reaching for his sword.

He had been raised in the Temple of the Silver Vigil, and the Moonwardens did not waste time wondering whether trouble might come to them. They went looking for it.

His family had served Selûne for generations, though service was perhaps too gentle a word for what had been expected of them. The Moonwardens were hunters, and their quarry was the cursed, the transformed, and those creatures that wore the shape of men until the moon called something darker from within them. He had been taught their trade almost as soon as he could walk. His childhood had been measured in lessons of tracking, weapons, restraint, and endurance, and by the time other boys were learning how to read, he was learning how to tell whether the blood upon a doorstep belonged to a man or a beast.

It had never seemed particularly strange to him.

It was simply what his family did.

Nor had he ever hated it. The work was dangerous, certainly, but it paid well enough to make the danger tolerable, and he had discovered early that there were worse things in the world than being well paid for doing something at which one was very good. He had an appreciation for good craftsmanship, and the work of a Moonwarden provided ample opportunity to acquire it. A fine sword was worth its price. Good armor could mean the difference between a scar and a funeral. A comfortable inn was preferable to a ditch, and a well-prepared meal was worth more than most sermons.

He had no shame about wanting such things.

He simply preferred a life in which he could afford them.

That life depended upon another lesson his family had taught him just as carefully as the others.

No one could know what he truly did.

The Moonwardens worked best when nobody knew they were present. A creature warned that a hunter was looking for it became difficult to find. A witch who knew her coven had been discovered did not remain where she could be found. The work required patience, deception, and the ability to walk into a town as an ordinary man and leave it before anyone realized why he had come.

So he had become an adventurer.

That was the name the world gave him, and he had never found reason to correct it.

He traveled, took contracts, explored old ruins, and spent his earnings rather more freely than most adventurers dared. He was a cleric of Selûne, or at least that much was true. What no one knew was that beneath the wandering life and the expensive equipment was a Moonwarden following trails that other men could not see.

It had been a comfortable arrangement.

Until the witches.

He remembered the battle now in pieces. He remembered the smell of rain upon the earth and the strange blue light that had burned between the trees. He remembered one of the witches laughing as though the fight were some private amusement, and he remembered the sudden pain in his shoulder when her magic struck him. He remembered thinking that she had missed him until he saw the mark the spell had left behind.

He had not understood what she had done.

Not then.

By the time he did, it was too late.

The curse had taken hold before dawn.

What followed became the reason he would never again be welcome beneath the roof of the Silver Vigil.

He had returned to Baldur's Gate believing that the temple could cleanse him. He knew the rites. He knew the prayers. He had watched them performed upon others and had assisted in more than one cleansing himself. Ordinary lycanthropy was not beyond the reach of the Moonwardens, and he had no reason to believe that his case would be different.

He had been wrong.

The priests had gathered around him beneath the silvered dome of the temple and called upon Selûne to drive the curse from his flesh. The first rite failed. The second did nothing. The third brought such agony that he nearly tore himself free of the restraints.

Then the beast came.

He remembered the faces of the priests.

That was what remained with him more clearly than anything else.

They had seen the wolf before. They had seen men change and had stood their ground while claws replaced fingers and human voices became snarls. They knew fear, but they knew how to master it.

This time they were looking at something they did not understand.

The witch's curse had twisted the lycanthropy into something beyond their knowledge. Their magic could not control it, and neither could he. When the transformation came upon him, there had been no prayer strong enough to stop it and no chain heavy enough to hold him.

The eastern quarter of Baldur's Gate had paid the price.

He had awakened afterward with blood upon his clothes and smoke filling the sky.

He did not know how many people had died. He had stopped asking himself that question because every answer was worse than the last. He remembered running through streets he had known since childhood, remembered men shouting his name, remembered Moonwardens closing in around him. He remembered recognizing every face.

These were the people who had taught him how to hunt.

They had come to hunt him.

He had escaped them only because he knew precisely how they would pursue him. Every trap they set was one he might once have set himself. Every formation they used was one he had practiced. He knew where they would expect him to run, and they knew he knew.

It was the closest thing to a fight between brothers that he had ever experienced.

When it was over, the elders of the Silver Vigil gave him his sentence.

Exile.

He had stood before them covered in the grime and blood of the night, while the priests spoke of what could not be controlled and what could not be permitted to happen again. They did not call him evil. They did not accuse him of choosing the beast. That almost made it worse.

They simply told him that he could no longer remain.

He had understood.

Had another Moonwarden returned to the temple carrying such a curse, he would have argued for exactly the same decision.

So he left.

Now, several days later, he rode hard along a forest road with the smoke of Baldur's Gate still visible behind him. His horse was exhausted, and so was he, but neither could afford to stop for long. He knew that the Moonwardens would eventually come after him. Perhaps they already had. He knew their methods too well to believe otherwise.

Yet he was not running simply to escape them.

He had a destination.

Before leaving the city, he had heard a rumor from a frightened merchant whose daughter had once disappeared into the western forests. The merchant had spoken of a coven that lived beyond the old ruins, witches who practiced forms of magic older than the temples and who dealt in curses that ordinary priests could not undo.

Under other circumstances, he might have been sent to kill them.

Now he intended to ask them for help.

The thought still struck him as absurd whenever it crossed his mind.

He had spent his entire life learning how to hunt witches, and now the survival of that life might depend upon finding one willing to speak with him. The priests of the Silver Vigil could not break the curse. Killing the witch who had placed it upon him would not remove it. Whatever had been woven into his blood would remain unless someone could find a way to unravel it.

And he was determined to find that someone.

Not because he feared becoming a monster. Fear had never been especially useful to him.

He simply had no intention of surrendering the life he had worked so hard to build.

He wanted his freedom. He wanted his coin. He wanted his fine weapons and comfortable rooms and the simple pleasure of knowing that, when he reached an inn at the end of a long journey, he could pay for the best room they had without looking at the price.

He wanted his life back.

Behind him, somewhere upon the road he had taken, another rider would eventually find the trail.

He knew who that rider would be.

One of the Moonwardens.

Someone trained in the same halls, beneath the same moon, by the same masters who had trained him. Someone who knew how he thought, how he traveled, how he concealed his tracks, and how he would respond when hunted.

Someone who understood that the man fleeing into the wilderness was not merely a cursed cleric.

He was a Moonwarden.

And he had been trained from birth to survive the hunt.
