## Chapter Two

# The Northern Road

By late afternoon he reached a small settlement farther along the road and stopped at an inn that looked clean enough to trust. The innkeeper was a broad-shouldered woman with a sharp eye and little patience for travelers who wanted to haggle over the price of a room. Bastian paid what she asked and took a seat near the hearth while he waited for supper. He did not mention the caravan.

The room was warmer than he expected, and for the first time in several days he allowed himself to stop moving without immediately considering when he would have to leave again. There were perhaps a dozen people in the common room, most of them locals, with a pair of teamsters arguing quietly over the price of grain and three woodsmen occupying a table near the window. Their conversation rose and fell beneath the clatter of bowls and the occasional crackle of the fire. Bastian listened without appearing to listen, which was a habit more useful than most people realized. Travelers talked when they were comfortable, and they talked even more when they believed the stranger beside them had no interest in what they were saying.

Supper arrived without ceremony. The innkeeper set a bowl of stew before him, followed by bread still warm from the kitchen, and gave him the briefest inspection before returning to her work. Bastian found the food better than he had expected and decided that the room had been worth the price. He ate slowly, letting the warmth settle into him while his attention wandered through the room. The settlement had the weary quiet of a place accustomed to seeing people pass through without expecting them to stay. A farmer came in long enough to collect a jug of ale. A boy carried a sack of flour through the rear door. One of the woodsmen complained that the northern bridge had lost another section to the spring floods, and his companion answered that it had been half broken since the thaw and would probably remain so until winter.

Bastian paid more attention to that than he meant to. He had been traveling north because of the caravan, and the road beyond the settlement was the only road that mattered to him now. A broken bridge would not stop him, but it might change the route of anyone who knew the country better than he did. He finished his meal and waited until the woodsmen had begun talking about something else before approaching the innkeeper.

“Is the northern road still open?”

She looked at him from behind the bar. “If you have a horse that knows how to swim.”

“That bad?”

“It gets worse after the bridge.”

Bastian considered this. “How far?”

“Depends where you're going.”

“I haven't decided.”

“You've got the look of a man who has.”

He smiled faintly. “What look is that?”

“The one men get when they don't want to tell an innkeeper their business.”

“I thought innkeepers preferred not to hear it.”

“Usually.”

She wiped the bar with a cloth and glanced toward the sword at his hip. “You one of those adventurers?”

“Something like that.”

“Then you're probably looking for work.”

“Not tonight.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Bastian raised his cup in surrender, and she gave a short laugh before moving away. He did not press her. There was little point in making himself memorable, and he had already learned that the people who lived beside lonely roads often knew more than they realized. Information came easier when it was allowed to arrive at its own pace.

It was not until later, when the room had thinned and the fire had burned low, that the innkeeper spoke to him again. She had been standing near the kitchen door, counting something on her fingers, when she stopped and looked across the room.

“You really are going north, aren't you?”

Bastian looked up from his drink. “I thought we settled that.”

“No. You avoided answering.”

“There's a difference?”

“For me, yes.”

He smiled. “Then yes. I'm going north.”

She nodded as though that confirmed something. “You should know that you're not the first stranger to ask about the road this week.”

That was more interesting.

Bastian set his cup down. “Who was the other?”

“A peddler.”

“What did he sell?”

“Nothing, apparently.”

Bastian waited.

“He had a cart,” she continued, “or said he did. I never saw it. Came through here five days ago and stayed the night. Asked about the road, the marsh, and an old mill farther north.”

“Which mill?”

“The one that burned.”

Bastian's expression did not change, though the answer sharpened his attention.

“How long ago?”

“Before my time.”

“Then why would a peddler be interested in it?”

“That's what I asked him.”

“And?”

“He said he wasn't.”

Bastian gave her a look.

She shrugged. “Some people are poor liars.”

“Did he say what he wanted at the mill?”

“No. He asked whether anyone had been there recently.”

“Had anyone?”

“Not that I knew of.”

“Anything else?”

The innkeeper thought for a moment. “He asked about travelers who had gone missing on the northern road.”

Bastian leaned back.

“Were there any?”

“There are always travelers who go missing somewhere.”

“Not what I asked.”

“No,” she said. “Not that I could remember.”

The answer was casual, but the woman had begun watching him more closely.

“You're interested.”

“I might be.”

“You know something?”

“Not yet.”

She studied him for another moment, then nodded toward the door. “The old road branches off beyond the marsh. Most people don't use it anymore. If you're going there, ask for Harlon at the ferry. He keeps a boat on the east bank when the water's low enough.”

“Does he know the mill?”

“He knows everything that happens north of here. Whether he understands any of it is another question.”

Bastian finished his drink.

“What happened to the peddler?”

“He left the next morning.”

“North?”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“As far as I saw.”

Bastian nodded and reached for his purse.

The innkeeper shook her head. “You've already paid.”

“Then consider this for the information.”

“I didn't give it to you for money.”

“I know.”

She frowned at the coin he left on the table but did not return it.

Bastian went upstairs not long afterward. He did not sleep immediately. The small piece of worked metal lay on the table beside the candle, and he turned it over once before putting it back into his pouch. He had no proof that it had anything to do with the caravan, the missing travelers, or the ruined mill. For all he knew, it had fallen from a merchant's wagon months ago and had nothing to do with any of it. Still, there were too many small things gathering along the same road to dismiss them all as coincidence.

He slept with the sword beside the bed.

The next morning he left before the settlement had fully woken.

The road narrowed almost at once. Fields gave way to scrub and then to thicker stands of alder and pine, and the ground became softer beneath the horse's hooves. Water appeared in the ditches on either side, dark and still beneath mats of green. The air smelled of wet earth and decaying leaves. Bastian kept his cloak fastened despite the warmth because the farther north he traveled, the more the wind seemed to carry the dampness with it.

He encountered few people. Once, late in the morning, he passed an old farmer leading two mules south. The man raised a hand in greeting but said nothing. A little farther on he found a broken cart abandoned beside the road. The wheels were gone, and whatever had once been carried inside had been removed. Bastian examined the ground but found only old tracks, most of them softened by rain.

Near midday he reached the bridge.

The innkeeper had not exaggerated. One section had collapsed completely, leaving a jagged gap above a broad stream swollen by recent rain. The remaining timbers looked strong enough to cross on foot, though Bastian was less certain about the horse. He dismounted, loosened the reins, and spent several minutes testing the boards before deciding there was no better route.

Halfway across, the horse slipped.

Bastian caught the reins before the animal could panic and steadied it while water rushed below them. For a moment he thought the whole bridge might give way. Then the horse found its footing, and they continued. When they reached the other side, Bastian let the animal rest beneath the trees while he looked back at the crossing.

Someone had crossed recently.

There were fresh hoofprints in the mud on the far bank.

Two horses, perhaps three.

He studied them for a moment and then looked north.

The road disappeared between the trees.

He mounted again.

By late afternoon the marsh had begun in earnest. Pools of black water spread between islands of dry ground, and twisted trees rose from the mud with their roots exposed. The road itself had become little more than a raised strip of earth winding between the water. Bastian slowed the horse and followed it carefully, watching for places where the ground had given way.

He found the ferry near sunset.

It was little more than a weathered platform beside the water with a small hut behind it. An old man sat outside repairing a net. When Bastian approached, the man looked up without surprise.

“Harlon?”

“If you're asking, you already know.”

“I was told you keep a ferry.”

“I keep a boat. Whether I keep a ferry depends on whether I like the person asking.”

Bastian smiled. “Then I'll try to be likable.”

“You've got a sword.”

“So I've been told.”

The old man looked him over, then returned to his net. “What do you want?”

“The old road.”

Harlon's hands stopped.

Only for a moment.

“There's no reason to go there.”

“So I've heard.”

“That usually means someone gave you a reason.”

“A peddler.”

The old man's eyes lifted.

Bastian noticed.

“Five days ago,” he said.

Harlon stared at him across the fading light.

Then he set the net aside.

“He asked about the mill.”

Bastian said nothing.

“And now you are.”

“I am.”

Harlon looked toward the marsh.

“The mill isn't there anymore.”

“I was told it burned.”

“It did.”

“Then what's the problem?”

The old man rubbed one hand across his beard. “The problem is that people keep asking about a place that hasn't existed for twenty years.”

Bastian glanced northward.

“Who keeps asking?”

Harlon did not answer immediately.

At last he said, “People who have business there.”

“That doesn't tell me much.”

“No. It doesn't.”

The old man rose slowly and walked toward the water. He stood at the edge of the platform, staring into the darkening marsh.

“You can cross in the morning,” he said. “Not tonight.”

“Why?”

“Because the marsh changes after dark.”

Bastian looked at him.

Harlon shrugged. “You can laugh if you like.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good. Then you're smarter than most.”

Bastian led the horse toward the shelter beside the hut.

He did not know what waited beyond the marsh, and he still had no idea whether the ruined mill had anything to do with the curse that had driven him from Baldur's Gate. But for the first time since leaving the city, he had found a trail that seemed to have been followed by someone else before him.

That was enough.

As he turned back toward the road, he caught the faintest movement among the trees on the southern bank.

Something pale flashed between the trunks.

He stopped.

The marsh wind stirred the branches, and the darkness settled again.

Bastian watched for several breaths before looking away.

There was nothing there.

He led the horse into the shelter and began making camp.
