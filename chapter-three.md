## Chapter Three

# The Marsh Road

Bastian was awake before the sun came up. He lay beneath the rough shelter beside Harlon's hut for several minutes, listening to the marsh breathe in the darkness. Water moved somewhere beyond the trees, insects stirred in the reeds, and his horse shifted its weight occasionally in the shelter beside him. Nothing seemed unusual, yet the feeling from the night before had not entirely left him. He had seen something on the southern bank, or thought he had, and experience had taught him that uncertainty was worth remembering even when it was not worth fearing. He dressed, checked his weapons, and went to wake the horse.

Harlon was already waiting at the ferry. The old man had a small fire going beside the hut and was drinking from a battered wooden cup when Bastian approached. “You're crossing,” he said without looking up.

“I am.”

Harlon studied him over the rim of his cup. “You could still turn around.”

“I could.”

The old man grunted. “You won't.”

Bastian smiled faintly. “Probably not.”

Harlon finished his drink and stood, moving toward the boat. “Then you'd better hope the marsh is in a good mood.”

The crossing was uneventful. From the water, the marsh looked larger than it had from the road, a broad expanse of dark pools and low islands broken by twisted trees and patches of reeds. Mist hovered just above the surface, turning the farther reaches into a gray wall. Old posts rose from the water at irregular intervals, remnants of some forgotten route through the wetland.

Bastian watched them as Harlon guided the boat across. “Were those part of the old road?”

Harlon nodded. “Some of them. There used to be more.”

“What happened?”

“The marsh took them.”

Harlon said it simply, as though that explained everything. Perhaps, in a place like this, it did.

When they reached the other side, Harlon secured the boat and pointed toward a narrow trail disappearing beneath the trees. “Follow that. You'll find the old road before noon if you don't get turned around.”

Bastian looked at the trail. “Does that happen often?”

“Often enough.” Harlon hesitated, then added, “Stay on the raised ground. The water isn't always as shallow as it looks.”

Bastian thanked him and started away, but after a few steps he stopped. “The peddler crossed here, didn't he?”

Harlon's expression tightened slightly. “Five days ago.”

“Did he go toward the mill?”

“He asked about it.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Harlon looked at him for a long moment. “He went north.”

Bastian waited, but the old man had nothing more to say. He mounted and followed the trail into the trees.

The marsh road appeared a little before midday. It was barely a road at all now, just a raised strip of packed earth winding between pools of black water and stands of alder. In places the original paving stones remained beneath the mud, worn smooth by years of rain and neglect. Bastian kept the horse to the center and moved slowly, watching for places where the ground had begun to collapse. The farther he went, the fewer signs of ordinary travel he found. There were no farms, no fences, no smoke from distant chimneys. Once he passed the remains of a small cart abandoned beside the road. Its wheels were gone, and whatever it had carried had been removed long ago. He stopped long enough to examine the ground, but the rain had softened the tracks beyond recognition.

Later that afternoon he found a fire ring beside the road. The ashes were old, but not old enough to have been left there for years. Bastian dismounted and crouched beside it, examining the ground around the stones. There were footprints, several of them, though the marsh had already begun to erase the edges. He followed one set a short distance toward the trees before losing it. Another continued along the road. A third seemed to turn toward the water and then simply vanished.

Bastian stood and looked across the marsh. He had spent enough years tracking things that did not want to be found to know that missing tracks did not always mean missing travelers. Sometimes they meant that someone had taken the trouble to hide them.

He continued north.

The old road divided near a stand of dead trees. One branch continued through the marsh while another climbed gradually toward firmer ground. A weathered sign leaned beside the northern path, its lettering nearly gone beneath moss. Bastian wiped the surface with his glove and found a single word still visible.

**MILL.**

He stared at it for a moment. The peddler had asked about the mill. Harlon had known exactly what he meant. The missing travelers, if the stories were worth anything, had disappeared somewhere along this road. It was enough to keep going.

By evening, the trees had begun to thin, and the marsh opened around the remains of the old mill. There was little left of the building. Four sections of blackened stone marked where the walls had once stood, and the remains of the wheel lay half buried beside a narrow stream. Trees had grown through the ruins, their roots pushing apart stones that had survived the fire.

Bastian dismounted and walked slowly through what remained, searching for anything that might tell him someone had been there recently. He found nothing. There were no fresh tracks, no discarded supplies, no sign of a camp. The mill had been dead for a long time.

He crouched beside the stream and turned over a piece of rusted iron before dropping it back into the mud. He had followed the trail this far only to discover that it led to a place that had apparently been empty for twenty years. He stood and looked over the ruins again, irritated more by the wasted time than by the lack of answers. The metal fragment from the caravan remained in his pouch, and for the first time he wondered whether he had given it too much importance. Perhaps it had nothing to do with the road at all.

He decided to camp rather than attempt the marsh after dark. There was a patch of dry ground farther along the road where the trees provided enough shelter from the wind, and Bastian led the horse there before gathering wood. He made a small fire and sat beside it while darkness settled around him. The day's work had left him tired, and the silence of the marsh seemed heavier now than it had in daylight.

He found himself thinking about Baldur's Gate, though he had been trying not to. The temple. His family. The cleansing chamber. The moment when the elders had realized that whatever had happened to him could not be undone by any rite they knew. He had spent every day since then moving forward because stopping meant thinking about what came next.

A sound came from the trees.

Bastian looked up. Nothing moved. He waited, one hand resting near the sword at his side, but the marsh returned to silence. After a moment he relaxed and leaned back against the tree.

The branches above him shifted slightly, and moonlight spilled through them.

He looked up.

The moon was full.

For several seconds he simply stared at it. Then his stomach tightened.

He had forgotten.

Not entirely. He knew the moon's cycle better than most people ever needed to. Every Moonwarden did. But the last several days had been a blur of roads, questions, bad sleep, and the constant knowledge that somewhere behind him another Moonwarden was following his trail. He had been thinking about the caravan, the peddler, the witch, the curse. He had been thinking about where to go next.

He had not been thinking about tonight.

Bastian stood slowly.

The first pain came before he could decide what to do. It was not the familiar sensation of ordinary lycanthropy. He knew that transformation too well. He had watched it happen in others, had studied its stages and learned the signs well enough to recognize them in darkness. This began deeper, somewhere beneath his ribs, and spread through him with a strange, deliberate pressure. Bastian gripped the tree and forced himself to breathe.

“No.”

His fingers tightened against the bark as the pain intensified. He reached for his sword but stopped halfway. His hand was already changing. His nails darkened and lengthened, but the transformation did not follow the pattern he expected. His bones shifted beneath his skin without the violent cracking he had witnessed in other cursed creatures. His muscles tightened, his senses sharpened, and something moved through him that felt less like an animal taking control than a second nature waking from sleep.

He dropped to one knee. The fire blurred in front of him, and the sounds of the marsh rushed together until he could hear everything at once—the movement of water beneath the reeds, the wings of something passing high above the trees, the small movements of creatures hidden beneath the roots. He tried to recite one of the prayers used during cleansing, but the words broke apart before he could finish them. Then, as suddenly as it had come, the pain was gone.

Bastian remained where he was for several moments, breathing heavily. When he finally looked at his hands, he knew at once that something was wrong. The change had not made him into the creature he knew. He was still recognizably himself, but there was something unnatural about the shape of him now. His fingers were longer, his nails dark and curved, and his body felt stronger without having grown larger. His senses were painfully acute, yet his thoughts remained clear.

That frightened him more than losing control would have. He had spent his entire life learning how to recognize lycanthropes. He knew what they were supposed to become. Whatever the witch had put inside him was not that.

Bastian rose and looked toward the ruins of the mill. Something smelled different. He could smell the water, the wet stone, the old ash buried beneath the soil. Beneath all of it was something faint that he had not noticed before, a stale scent of smoke and iron coming from somewhere beneath the ruins.

He walked toward it, and the darkness no longer concealed as much as it had. Details seemed to separate themselves from the shadows. What had looked like ordinary stone during the day now showed scratches and marks along its surface. Bastian moved around the remains of the mill until he reached the old foundation, where he noticed a narrow opening between two stones beneath a layer of wet leaves. He crouched and cleared it with one hand.

A mark had been carved into the stone beneath: a circle, broken in several places, with three narrow lines inside it.

Bastian stared at it before reaching into his pouch and pulling out the piece of worked metal from the caravan. He held it beside the carving, and the resemblance was unmistakable. It was not the same object, nor was the design identical, but the same hand had made them, or someone who knew the same symbols.

He looked back toward the ruins. The mill had not been the destination. It had been a marker.

Somewhere in the marsh, something called out in the darkness. The sound was distant, low, and impossible to identify. Bastian turned toward it, listening. For an instant he thought he saw movement between the trees, a pale shape slipping between the trunks, but when he looked again there was nothing there.

He remained beneath the full moon, listening to the marsh with senses he had never possessed before. The trail he had been following had not ended at the ruined mill. If anything, the discovery beneath its foundation had made the road ahead less certain than it had been before. Whatever the witch had done to him, whatever the mark meant, someone had left it here for a reason.

Bastian looked once more toward the darkness beyond the ruins and tightened his grip on the metal fragment.

The trail had only just begun.
